10 mind-bending illusions, and why your brain falls for them

1. Jastrow arcs

Which curve is bigger? They are the same shape and the same size.

Your brain compares the two edges that sit closest together: the short inner curve of the top block against the long outer curve of the bottom one. That local comparison makes the top block look smaller. Named after Joseph Jastrow (1892).

2. Ponzo rails

The two yellow bars are exactly the same length.

The converging rails look like a road or a pair of tracks going into the distance. Your brain treats the upper bar as farther away. The same length on the screen is then read as a longer object in the world. Mario Ponzo described this in 1911.

3. Müller-Lyer arrows

Both horizontal shafts are 180 units long. Only the arrowheads change.

The fins bias where the shaft seems to end. Outward fins make the line look longer; inward fins tuck the ends in. One common reading is that the fins look like corners in depth, so the “farther” shaft is scaled up. Franz Müller-Lyer published it in 1889.

4. Café wall

Every mortar line is perfectly horizontal. The rows only look sloped.

The thin grey “mortar” sits between dark and light tiles that are shifted by half a step on each row. Contrast pushes the apparent edge of each tile up or down, so the mortar looks like a zigzag. Richard Gregory and Priscilla Heard named the effect in 1979, after tiles on a café in Bristol; a related pattern was already described in the 1890s.

5. Kanizsa triangle

There is no white triangle drawn here — only three discs with a bite taken out. Your brain supplies the edges.

When several shapes look “bitten” by the same hidden object, the visual system invents sharp illusory contours and even a brighter surface in the middle. Gaetano Kanizsa made this triangle famous in 1955. It is a reminder that seeing is construction, not a camera feed.

6. Fraser spiral

Try to trace one “arm” of the spiral with your finger. You will land on a circle.

Every path in this figure is a concentric circle. The twisted black-and-white stitches lean the same way, and your brain joins those local twists into one long spiral. James Fraser described the effect in 1908. It is a close cousin of the Zöllner illusion, where short hatch marks make straight lines look tilted.

7. Poggendorff line

The two black stumps belong to one straight line. The bar only hides the middle.

Your visual system is not great at continuing an oblique line behind an occluder. Acute angles are often mis-read, so the far stump looks too high or too low. Johann Poggendorff noticed the effect in 1860, on a drawing of another illusion.

8. Vertical–horizontal

The upright stroke and the base are the same length: 180 units.

In this inverted T, the vertical line usually looks longer. One reason is that a vertical line is often overestimated compared with a horizontal one of the same length; the T junction also “eats” a little of the base, so the horizontal line can look shorter. The effect goes back to work by Adolf Fick in the mid-19th century, and later Wilhelm Wundt.

9. Shepard tables

Both tops are the same parallelogram. One is just turned.

Once the shapes are read as tabletops in a 3-D room, “how long is this table?” is answered in world coordinates, not on the page. A parallelogram that is tall on the screen looks like a short, deep table; the same parallelogram lying on its side looks long and narrow. Roger Shepard popularised the demonstration in 1981 as “Turning the Tables”. The drawing here is our own.

10. Peripheral drift

Look at the centre, then notice the corners. Any motion you see is in your visual system, not in the file. (If you asked your device to reduce motion, the pattern stays still and may look weaker.)

Repeating steps of dark and light, arranged in a loop, can be read as motion — especially in the corner of your eye, or while you move your gaze. This is the Fraser–Wilcox / peripheral-drift effect. The pinwheels on this page are an original, simple pattern. They are not a copy of anyone else’s artwork.

Illusions are features, not bugs. Your brain is doing what it evolved to do: guess depth, lighting, and movement from a flat pattern of light. These figures push those guesses until they disagree with a ruler.
